Contractor Job Costing: How to Know What a Job Actually Made

If the only number you know after a job is what you charged, you still do not know what the job made.

The four numbers that matter

A basic job-cost review compares the price you sold the work for against what the job actually consumed.

  1. Contract value: the agreed selling price, including approved change orders.
  2. Estimated total cost: what you expected materials, labor, subcontractors, equipment and other direct costs to consume.
  3. Actual total cost: what those same categories really cost when the work was done.
  4. Gross profit: contract value minus actual job cost. Gross margin is gross profit divided by contract value.

That comparison is more useful than simply asking whether there was cash left in the bank. Cash timing, deposits and bills can make a job feel profitable or unprofitable before the final numbers are known.

What to track on every job

Materials

Track supplier purchases, delivery, returns, waste and the small purchases that happen after the original order.

Labor

Track actual hours, not just installation hours. Setup, material handling, pickup runs, cleanup and callbacks consume time too.

Other direct costs

Include disposal, equipment, rentals, subcontractors, permits when applicable, fuel or other job-specific costs that would not exist without that project.

Approved changes

Update contract value and cost when scope changes. Otherwise the final margin comparison is measuring the wrong job.

A simple example

A job sells for $10,000. You estimated $3,000 material, $3,000 labor and $500 other direct cost, for $6,500 estimated job cost.

Estimated gross profit: $3,500. Estimated gross margin: 35%.

At completion, materials were $3,400, labor was $3,600 and other direct costs were $600. Actual job cost is $7,600.

Actual gross profit: $2,400. Actual gross margin: 24%.

The job still made gross profit, but it gave up $1,100 compared with the estimate. The useful next question is where: material, labor, scope, rework, or another cost category?

Common job-costing mistakes

  • Tracking only big material orders and ignoring later supply runs.
  • Using estimated labor hours after the job instead of recording actual hours.
  • Counting revenue from a change order without adding the cost of doing the extra work.
  • Mixing company-wide overhead with direct job costs without a consistent method.
  • Reviewing only jobs that obviously went bad instead of every completed job.
  • Looking at profit dollars without margin percentage, which makes jobs of different sizes hard to compare.
