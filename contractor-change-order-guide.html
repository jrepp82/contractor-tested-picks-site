Contractor Change Orders: Price Extra Work Before It Becomes Free Work

Small additions are where a profitable estimate can slowly turn into an unprofitable finished job.

What a useful change order should answer

  1. What changed? Describe added, removed or substituted scope clearly.
  2. Why did it change? Customer request, concealed condition, design revision, quantity change or another documented reason.
  3. What does it cost? Include the material, labor, equipment, subcontractor and other costs caused by the change, then price it using the same business method used on the original estimate.
  4. What happens to the schedule? Note added time, material lead time or sequencing impact when it matters.
  5. Who approved it? Preserve written approval before proceeding whenever practical.

The dangerous sentence: “While you're here…”

Many expensive change orders start as small favors. One extra repair becomes another material run. A minor opening exposes damage. A customer changes a finish after materials were ordered. None of those automatically mean conflict—but they do mean the original scope and price may no longer describe the job being performed.

The goal of a change-order process is not to make the relationship adversarial. It is to keep the customer and contractor working from the same scope, price and expectations.

Simple change-order workflow

1. Stop and document

Write down what is different before the extra work blends into the original job.

2. Price the difference

Calculate added or removed material, labor and other direct costs. Do not price from memory when a real quantity or supplier cost is available.

3. State the impact

Show the change-order amount and the revised contract total. Note schedule effects when applicable.

4. Get approval

Preserve the customer's written acceptance under the contract and local rules that apply to your work.

5. Update job cost

Add the new revenue and the new expected cost so the job's margin is still measured against the actual scope.

Common leaks

  • Doing the work first and discussing price after.
  • Charging only for added material and forgetting added labor, handling or disposal.
  • Adding revenue to the contract but failing to update the job-cost budget.
  • Using vague wording that does not say what is included or excluded.
  • Failing to document a schedule impact created by the added scope.
  • Treating every small change as “included” until dozens of small changes become real unpaid labor.
