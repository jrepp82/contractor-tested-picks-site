Pricing fundamentals

Contractor Labor Rate: Why Your Hourly Wage Is Not Your Billable Rate

If you charge customers the same number you want to earn per hour, the business is paying the rest of the job for free.

Start with the difference between wage and billable rate

A wage or owner's desired hourly pay is only one cost of putting skilled labor on a job. The customer-facing labor rate also has to support the business costs that make that hour possible.

A practical rate model starts with four buckets:

  1. Direct labor cost: wages or owner compensation assigned to production time.
  2. Labor burden: payroll taxes, workers compensation, benefits or other employment-related costs when applicable.
  3. Business overhead: insurance, vehicles, tools, shop/office, software, non-billable time and other operating costs.
  4. Profit: the return left after the business costs are covered—not a substitute for paying for your own labor.

The productive-hours problem

A contractor may work 50 hours in a week without having 50 customer-billable production hours. Estimates, supplier runs, scheduling, collections, maintenance, callbacks, bookkeeping and travel all consume real time.

If annual overhead is divided by every hour you are awake and working instead of the hours customers can realistically be billed for, the hourly overhead allowance can look artificially low.

Simple concept: annual business cost ÷ realistic annual billable hours = cost that each billable hour must help recover.

Illustrative example

This is only an example, not a recommended market rate.

  • Desired direct labor compensation: $40/hour
  • Labor burden allocation: $8/hour
  • Annual overhead to recover: $48,000
  • Realistic billable hours: 1,200/year

Overhead recovery per billable hour: $48,000 ÷ 1,200 = $40/hour.

Before profit, the illustrative business needs $88/hour just to cover the $40 labor, $8 burden and $40 overhead allocation. A sustainable selling rate would still need to address the business's chosen profit method.

The important lesson is not the $88 number. It is that a $40 desired wage and a $40 billable rate are completely different things.

Common labor-pricing mistakes

Using a wage as the rate

The business then absorbs payroll burden, overhead and non-billable time.

Ignoring owner labor

Owner labor is still a real production input. Treating it as “free” makes job profit look better than it is.

Using impossible billable hours

Overhead recovery looks cheaper when the denominator assumes every work hour is directly billable.

Adding overhead twice

If a standard labor rate already recovers overhead, blindly adding the same overhead again can inflate a quote. Know exactly where each cost is recovered.

Calling markup profit

Markup and margin are not the same calculation. Whatever method you use, reconcile the final selling price against all costs.
